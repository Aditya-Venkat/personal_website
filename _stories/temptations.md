“Excuse me, is this seat taken?”
I looked up, startled out of my musings, at the source of the dialogue.
A girl stood before me, a stranger, about 5 feet tall.
>When I saw her the winds stopped blowing. The air around me gave such a revolting smell, I felt like puking. She had an eerie vibe to her no-one but I could make out.<
She was dressed in a cheap-looking orange crop top, a tattered denim jacket and torn black jeans. Her face was caked with grime and dirt, and her skin clung tightly to her bones.  Mascara ran down from her eyes in a river of ink, and the ring on her bottom lip shone in the bright lighting of the classroom. Her sharp eyes peered at me through the curtain of her frizzy, black hair.
“Go ahead,” I gestured at the seat in front of me, somewhat hesitantly.
She sat down, legs spread in a manner society finds distasteful, and placed her elbows behind her, on my desk. Tapping her feet on the ground, she began humming a strange tune. A tune that seemed to disobey any law of tempo or melody. A tune that seemed foreign, yet eerily familiar. Shivers. Her scent was stronger now. I gagged, and switched to mouth breathing. I looked at her with disdain. I could see lice crawling through her hair. Ugh.
The classroom continued to buzz with activity. A chalk scratching formulae on a blackboard. The steady buzz of hushed conversation. Occasional laughter, followed by the furious turn-back of the teacher and immediate silence. 
The girl leaned back, her head now on my table, looking at me upside down. 
“You feeling better now?”
“Hmm?”
“Your head. You said it was hurting.”
“I did?” I didn’t remember that. But I seemed to be forgetting more and more things recently. I wasn’t too concerned though. “Uh, it's better now… thanks for asking.”
The girl sat up and turned back. She leaned forward and placed her hand on mine. 
“You don’t have to be strong for me, you know?  I’m here for you. Whenever you need me. Just talk to me.” She picked up my hand and held it in hers.
“Uh, I’m sorry.” I retracted my hand, “Do we know each other?”
The girl cocked her head. She sat back again, a mock pout on her lips.
“Now, now,” She chided. “You couldn’t have possibly forgotten me. After all the sleepless nights we’ve shared together?”
She stood up and placed a palm on my cheek. “It’s been a while, sure, but I know you’ll come back to me.”
She bent forward, placing her lips against my ear lobe. 
“You always have.”

That night at dinner, I wasn’t hungry.
As the day went on, I had tried finding her again, but we didn’t have any classes together. Anyone I had described her to had had no idea who I was talking about. She was, by far, the most repulsive person I have ever met, and yet I wanted to see her again. I NEEDED to see her again. The more time passed, the more I craved her presence. 
I toyed with my pasta, picking it up with my fork, peering through the cavity of the penne, and letting it drop. My mother watched me in concern. I glanced at her, as I brought the fork up to my mouth. 
Hair unkempt, dirty clothes, eye-bags. The beautiful woman, the diamond desired to be held by every man who laid eyes on her, whom even straight women found themselves staring at was long gone, buried with my father. My mother hadn’t been my mother for a long time, but for that matter I hadn’t been myself either. Dad’s death had been hard on both of us.
“Sweetie, why don’t you eat a little? You need your energy.”
“I’m not hungry.” 
Her eyes stayed fixed on me, searching my face for any sign of emotion, anything that would help her understand what was going on in my head. Or in my life. But I couldn't let her know. The poor woman had suffered enough. Besides, I had it under control. 
I stood up.
“Good night, mom.”
She opened her mouth to say something, but then closed it again. She placed her face in her hands, rubbing her eyes with her thumbs, stifling obvious exhaustion, as I went up the stairs back to my room.
Thoughts of the girl began to flood my thoughts again, as I trudged through the hallway. Oh how I wanted to see her again. Every instinct in me told me not to seek her out and yet - I stopped and rested a hand on the wall. 
Sigh… I closed my eyes for a second.
My vision began glazing over. Probably just drowsiness. I rubbed my eyes. And just as I opened them, the hallway began stretching out. The walls leaned in menacingly.
What in the - 
My heart began to race. 
“Calm down, you’re just tired,” I told myself.
The floor began to wobble. I began running, panting. My heart pounded heavily in my chest. My mind was racing. The paintings on the wall reached out towards me, trying to grab me. I ducked, weaved and bolted towards my bedroom door, almost losing my balance.
Shoving it open, I leaped in and fell flat, face-down on my bed. Hugging my pillow, I gasped. 
Breathe…. Breathe…
I reached over to the laptop next to me and put on a song I had reserved for just an occasion. Hotel California by the Eagles, the song my dad used to play whenever I was sick. It always calmed me down. I closed my eyes and let the music capture me, allowing me to forget myself in the slew of notes, my consciousness dissipating, becoming one with the melody. 
“You told me you were okay,” I heard a voice say suddenly.
I sat up, and turned. 
My window was open and on the sill sat a very familiar person. It was her.
She slipped into my room. “Clearly you aren’t.”
She shook her head disapprovingly. Her repugnant scent filled the room.
“Just do it. We both know you want this.”
My eyes stayed glued to her, as I felt the beginnings of a desire stir in me, as it had many times before. A desire I knew would soon turn to craving, and then uncontrollable lust.
“No I don’t,” I replied. “I’ll never do it again. I – I can’t.” I looked her in the eye. “I won’t.”
“You will.”
She walked over to me, coming around my bed. Watching me as she circled, she spoke.
“When your dad died, you turned to me. I saved your life. I took you in my arms and numbed your pain. And now… now you think you don’t need me anymore.” She stopped, having reached my bedside. “Now you think you’re done with me.”
She flashed a sinister smile. She climbed up onto my bed, placing one leg on either side of me, and then sat, straddling me. She leaned closer, her eyes glowing dangerously.
“But I’m not done with you. Don’t you see? It’s just like that song you always listen to, the one you have on right now. You can check out any time you like-”
 Reaching into my drawer she pulled out a tiny cylindrical case. Harmless looking to most. But I knew what it was. She popped it open and tilted it, spilling out 4 pills onto her right palm.
“- but you can never leave.”
She knelt forward, lying down on top of me. Her body pressing against mine, she held her right hand out next to my head, palm open.
Temptation. I couldn’t let my habit consume me again. It had done enough damage already; I couldn’t let it destroy everything I had left. I looked at the girl. A demon. Dependence in human form.
I began to tremble, anxiety taking hold of me. Beads of sweat formed all over my face and body. My breath became shaky and my arms and legs began to tingle. 
Then pain. Acute pain. I tried to scream but the girl shut my mouth with her left palm. I tried to resist. I knew it would be better soon, if I could just brave the pain. But it was too much. Way too much. 
I closed my eyes and let yearning take over. The girl smiled.
“I told you you’d come back to me.”
She dropped the 4 pills into my mouth and leaned closer, her lips almost touching mine. 
“Now we’re together again.”
The pills began to extend their unholy tendrils all through my body, as I started to doze off. The music playing in the background began distorting, notes stretching out, chords becoming asynchronous until it transformed into a very familiar tune. It was the tune I had heard the girl sing in the morning, which to me, announced the breaking of my frail spirit. I had lost. Again.
Every night I saw her. The angel of death. The harbinger of my doom. Every night I tried to resist her and every night I succumbed.  I hated myself. I hated my dirty clothing. I hated my ragged appearance. I hated the man I saw in the mirror every night, seeking solace in the girl’s filthy embrace. Unable to let go. Unable to quit.

I hadn’t showered in weeks now.
I reeked. I reeked of the very odour I had been repulsed by, those many days ago. The scent of the girl.
I shut my eyes and clenched my teeth, tears streaming down my cheeks. My life was in shambles.
Mom had found out. She had caught me stealing money out of her purse and had stormed into my room. What she saw had broken her heart. 
Empty cases covered the floor. Pills lay scattered across the bed and all over the side-stand and desk drawer. 
My father’s death had broken her, but I had been the one to finally put her down.
She no longer spoke to me, nor anyone. She didn’t work, she didn’t do chores, she didn’t go outside. She sat all day at the kitchen table, staring out the window, her mouth slightly agape and her eyes devoid of any emotion. Her body now a shallow husk, no longer host to a soul. 
I had no friends anymore. All I had was the girl. The girl gave me comfort. The girl took away my anxiety and my pain. She didn’t ask for anything in return. The girl didn’t judge, she understood. She didn’t expect anything from me. She knew the hole my father’s death had left in my heart, and she knew how to fill it, if only fleetingly.
But there was no girl. I knew that. 
I had always known that.
There was only me. And my problem.
The problem I’ve had since my dad’s death. The problem I once thought would be a solution. The problem that had eaten away my life and everything in it. 
I opened my eyes and looked down at my mother’s purse. The last of the money we had was in it. I looked over at my mother, sitting where she always does, at the kitchen table, watching the children play outside. She’s probably remembering the time I was that age. So innocent and full of life. The brave little boy she had always thought would make her proud. 
It killed me to know how badly I had let her down.
I reached into her purse, sobbing now. How many times had I stolen from this purse to fund my habit? I had lost count long ago. 
But this time it was different. This would be the last time I ever steal from my mother.
I grabbed a wad of cash, not even bothering to be discreet. Opening the door, I walked out onto the sidewalk. I held out a hand, hailing for a cab. A few minutes later, one stopped.
“Where to sir?”
“Sunny Day Rehabilitation Centre, please.”
I was done. Done with the girl. For now, and forever. 
It felt as if I had already lost everything. But I had to try to pick up the scraps and rebuild myself. For me and for my mother.
I looked out at the buildings zipping past me. The busy streets were filled with people. People with lives. People who had other people they loved and who loved them. People with places to go and things to do. The glimmer of hope of being one among them again excited me, just a tiny bit. And I felt a tinge of optimism. 
I swore on my father’s spirit to never stop fighting. To never give in to treacherous temptation ever again. I swore that I would turn over a new leaf and I knew in my very core that this time, I would not lose. This time Hotel California would play, unperturbed and undistorted. 
The cab slowed down at a red light. The driver cursed under his breath and sounded the horn loudly. 
“I’m sorry sir, looks like we’re going to be late”
“It’s alright, I’ve got my whole life ahead of me.”
I looked out my window again. Watching with silent delight, the birds chirping and circling the skies and the squirrels scuttling up a tree, chittering and playing. 
As the signal turned green and the cars began to move, I caught a glimpse of what seemed to be the girl, standing in an alley, looking straight at me. I smiled at her and waved. A goodbye I hoped would be final.
The girl turned away from me and disappeared into the shadows, taking with her a chapter of my life I hoped to never see again.
